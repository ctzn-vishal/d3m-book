Most analytics looks data-driven and isn't. A team builds a dashboard tracking weekly sales by region. The chart is beautiful. The numbers are accurate. The colours are tasteful. This article draws the line between the two. The distinction is small enough to define in three rules and large enough that almost every analytics failure in a modern firm sits on the wrong side of it. ## The Executive Question

> *What separates a decision that is data-driven from a decision that is merely data-decorated?*

The honest answer is three rules. Decisions are data-driven when:

1.  **The action is named.** A specific lever a specific person could pull.
2.  **The counterfactual is named.** What would happen if we did not act, or acted differently.
3.  **The threshold for acting is named.** The minimum effect size, ROI, or signal we would need to commit.

Anything missing any of those three is description in disguise. It may be smart description. It may be useful description. But it is not yet a decision.

## Three Ways the Distinction Breaks Down

Three failure patterns recur. None of them is a methods problem. All of them are framing problems.

**Failure 1: action without comparison.** A team observes that customers who received a marketing email spent more than customers who did not. They roll out the email campaign. They've optimized for a metric — *spend among recipients* — that always rises when the firm contacts its best customers. The comparison they needed — *what those same customers would have done without the email* — never appeared. Part III is largely about why this happens and how to fix it.

**Failure 2: prediction without action.** A team builds a high-AUC churn model. It's accurate. It's well-calibrated. It's deployed. No retention team owns the action the score is supposed to trigger. The model produces a score every Monday morning that goes into nobody's queue. The model is correct. The decision is missing. Part IV opens with this failure.

**Failure 3: AI without governance.** A team ships a customer-facing chatbot that answers from the firm's documents. Six weeks later it confidently quotes a refund policy that doesn't exist. The team is shocked. The model wasn't asked to refuse; the documents weren't versioned; nobody monitored grounding rate. Part V's last chapter is the playbook against this failure.

Each failure can be diagnosed by reading the three rules backwards: which one was missing?

## The Decision Question Card (Preview)

The artefact that holds the three rules together appears for the first time in §9.1. It is a six-line card a sponsor and an analyst fill in together before any data work begins:

1.  **Action.** The specific intervention under managerial control.
2.  **Outcome.** The business metric that should move.
3.  **Unit of analysis.** The level at which the action is applied and measured.
4.  **Timing and horizon.** When the intervention starts; the window over which the outcome is measured.
5.  **Counterfactual comparison.** What would have happened to the same units in the absence of the action.
6.  **Threshold for acting.** The minimum effect that would justify acting.

A team that can fill this card in a fifteen-minute meeting has the bones of a defensible analytics project. A team that cannot is not yet ready to fit a model. The card grows up across the book: it becomes the Predictive Task Contract in §14.2, the Model Card in §15.5, the AI Workflow Card in §22.1, and the Decision Memo in §24.1. We will trace the family in §0.2.

## The Decision Ladder

The book's spine is a sequence of decision questions, each one harder than the last and each one requiring a new evidence language to answer.

Reading the ladder:

- **Rungs 1–2 (Parts I–II).** Description and visual comparison. The work of seeing what happened, where, and for whom.
- **Rungs 3–4 (Part III).** Causal designs and regression. The work of separating "what we see" from "what we caused."
- **Rung 5 (Part IV).** Prediction. The work of saying who or what will likely do what next.
- **Rung 6 (Part V).** AI workflows on unstructured data. The work of reading text, images, and documents the structured methods cannot see.
- **Rung 7 (Part VI).** The system view. The work of running the analytics organization as a learning system rather than a sequence of one-off projects.

The ladder is climbed; it is not skipped. Every rung uses what was learned on the rungs below. The book is structured so that a question at any rung can be traced down to the description it rests on and up to the operation it informs.

The recurring questions Bean &amp; Basket faces are the questions the ladder climbs:

- *What's happening in the Southeast region's app sentiment this month?* (Part I–II)
- *Did our last pastry promotion cause a margin lift, or just attract customers who would have come anyway?* (Part III)
- *Which customers are most likely to churn in the next sixty days, and what offer would change their mind?* (Part IV)

Five questions, five Parts, one company. The methods will rotate. The firm will not.

## What This Book Asks of the Reader

A few habits the book will keep coming back to. Forming them now will save the reader effort throughout:

1.  **Write the brief before fitting the model.** If you can't write the Decision Question Card, the modelling work isn't ready.
2.  **Read the visual first.** Every method in the book has a default chart; learn the chart before the equation.
3.  **Treat methods as decision aids, not rituals.** Methods earn their place by improving a specific decision. If they don't, they don't ship.
4.  **Distinguish association, causation, prediction, recommendation, and automation.** They look similar. They aren't.
5.  **End with an artefact.** Cards, memos, model cards, dashboards, studios — every Part ships something durable.

These are not stylistic preferences. They are how the book intends to be useful long after the page closes.

## Managerial Takeaway

The line between data-decorated and data-driven is narrow and consequential. Three rules — name the action, name the counterfactual, name the threshold — separate them. Everything in the book is downstream of that distinction.

> *If the analysis cannot finish three sentences — "we propose this action, compared to this alternative, and we will act if the effect exceeds this threshold" — it is not yet a decision.*
