Every chapter so far has assumed a human at the controls. A person framed the question, wrote the SQL, fit the model, read the chart, and signed the memo. The methods changed; the operator did not. This final part asks what changes when the operator is an AI agent — when a system can pull data from the warehouse, run an analysis, watch a model for drift, and draft the recommendation, calling on the human only at the moments that matter. The word *agent* has been stretched to cover everything from a chatbot to a science-fiction android, so the first task is to pin it down. The distinction that has held up — and that the major labs now share — is about who controls the workflow.

## Workflow or agent?

Anthropic's widely cited guidance draws the line cleanly: a **workflow** is a system where models and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths, while an **agent** is a system where the model dynamically directs its own process and tool use, keeping control over how it accomplishes the task.<Cite id="anthropic-agents" sources={sources} /> OpenAI's framing agrees from the other side — an agent is a system that *independently accomplishes tasks on your behalf*; an app that calls a model but does not let it control execution (a simple chatbot, a single-turn classifier) is not an agent at all.<Cite id="openai-guide" sources={sources} />

The difference matters for managers because it changes what you are buying and what can go wrong. A workflow is bounded and testable: it does the same five steps every time. An agent is flexible and open-ended: it can handle a question you didn't anticipate — and it can also wander somewhere you didn't intend. Most production systems are still workflows with a little agency sprinkled in. The frontier is moving toward genuine agents, and the rest of this part is about that frontier.

## What a data agent is made of

Strip away the marketing and a data agent has a recognizable anatomy. At the center is a language model — the reasoning core. Around it sit four augmentations: **tools** (the ability to run SQL, search, or execute code), **memory** (state that persists across turns), **retrieval** (access to documents and the firm's semantic layer), and **planning** (the ability to decompose a goal and reflect on progress). What turns these parts into an agent is the *loop*: the model plans, acts on the world through a tool, observes the result as ground truth, reflects, and decides what to do next — stopping when the goal is met or when it is time to ask a human.<Cite id="anthropic-agents" sources={sources} />

## The autonomy dial

The most important managerial idea in this part is that *autonomy is a setting, not a property*. The same model can be deployed to merely suggest, to draft-and-wait, to act only after a human approves, or to run a routine loop unsupervised. Autonomy is co-determined by the model, the human oversight wrapped around it, and the product design — which means it is something you choose, deliberately, per task.

## Where the market actually is

It is easy to mistake the volume of agent announcements for the volume of agent deployment. The honest picture is a wide experiment-to-scale gap. McKinsey's 2025 survey found that while 88% of organizations use AI regularly and 62% are at least experimenting with agents, only 23% are scaling an agent in even one function — and fewer than 10% are scaling agents in any single function. Just 39% report enterprise-level profit impact.<Cite id="mckinsey" sources={sources} />

The trajectory, though, is steep. Gartner expects 40% of enterprise applications to embed task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026, up from under 5% a year earlier;<Cite id="gartner-apps" sources={sources} /> Deloitte projected the share of generative-AI adopters running agentic pilots to climb from a quarter in 2025 toward half by 2027.<Cite id="deloitte" sources={sources} /> And the data platforms are already reporting scale: within three months of launch, Snowflake said more than a thousand customers had deployed over fifteen thousand AI agents on its Intelligence product.<Cite id="snowflake-intel" sources={sources} />

<Pitfall title="Agent washing and autonomy theater">
Because &ldquo;agent&rdquo; sells, vendors slap the label on ordinary workflows and dashboards. Gartner went so far as to estimate that of thousands of vendors claiming agentic AI, only about 130 offer the real thing. **Before believing an &ldquo;agent,&rdquo; ask the workflow-or-agent question: does the model actually choose its own steps and tools, or is it filling blanks in a fixed script?** The honest answer is usually the latter — which is fine, as long as you are not paying agent prices for workflow capability.
</Pitfall>

The same forces that drive adoption also drive disappointment: Gartner forecasts that more than 40% of agentic-AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, undone by cost, unclear value, and weak controls.<Cite id="gartner-cancel" sources={sources} /> That is not a reason to stay out. It is a reason to bring discipline — the kind this book has spent seventeen chapters building.

<Takeaway label="The frame for Part VI">

An agent is a model that controls its own workflow; a data agent is that model augmented with tools, memory, retrieval, and planning, running a loop over the firm's data. The capability is real and arriving fast, but it is shallow in production and surrounded by hype. The manager's job is not to chase autonomy but to set it deliberately — task by task, on a dial whose position is a risk decision. The chapters that follow survey the four capabilities that make agentic analytics work, and the governance that keeps it safe.

</Takeaway>
