Part V is about pulling that evidence into the same decision loop. The path is short: classical text analysis, then embeddings, then language models, then agents and governance. But the first move — the one this article is about — is to stop treating "unstructured" as a synonym for "unusable" and start treating it as data that needs **a representation layer**.

---

## The Executive Question

> What business claim is this body of text, document, or image capable of supporting — and how does it become evidence a model can use?

The honest version: a review, taken raw, supports no claim a database can act on. Once it is converted into a representation — tokens, TF-IDF, an embedding, a structured extraction — it supports many.

## The Representation Move

Every method in this part begins with the same move: turn the raw input into something a model can compute on.

For text:

- **Bag-of-words / TF-IDF.** A document becomes a sparse vector of word counts. Cheap, transparent, the bedrock of §18.3–§18.5.
- **Embeddings.** A document becomes a dense vector in a learned meaning space. The bridge from classical to modern (§19).
- **LLM measurement.** A document is read by a language model that returns scores or labels on managerially defined constructs. The conceptual heart of the part (§19.2).

For images:

- **CNN features.** An image becomes a hierarchy of edges → textures → object parts → objects (§20.2).
- **Image embeddings.** An image becomes a vector in the same space as text — the basis for multimodal search (§20.4).

For documents:

- **OCR + layout.** A scanned page becomes text plus spatial structure (§20.3).
- **Schema-driven extraction.** A document plus a JSON schema becomes a set of validated fields (§21.2).

The representation determines which questions become askable. Bag-of-words can answer "which words are most overrepresented in negative reviews?" An embedding can answer "what are customers angry about, even if they don't use the word *angry*?" An LLM measurement can answer "do these customers feel betrayed?" These are three different questions, and they require three different representations.

  Unstructured does not mean unusable. It means there is one extra step — representation — before the rest of the decision loop kicks in.
</Callout>

## What Can Go Wrong

Three failures recur whenever a team first tries to add text or images to their models.

1.  **Confusing classification with measurement.** A text classifier returns a label ("complaint", "praise"); a measurement returns a degree on a defined construct ("intent to return", "sense of betrayal"). They are different operations. Section 19.3 is about not collapsing them.
2.  **Treating representation as neutral.** Every preprocessing choice — lowercasing, stop-word removal, lemmatization, chunking — encodes a hypothesis about what the text "is." A model that processes "cold brew" the same way it processes "cold coffee" has made an assumption, not a measurement.
3.  **Outsourcing the construct.** Off-the-shelf sentiment classifiers measure *general-domain sentiment*. Whether that is the right construct for the firm's question depends on the business, not the model. The recurring rule: name what you want to measure first, then choose the method.

## Managerial Takeaway

The shortest summary of this part: **Part IV scored customers. Part V reads them.** A managerial frame to hold throughout:

> *Name the unstructured source. Choose the representation. State the construct. Pick the method. Inspect failures. Govern the output.*

The next five articles work through that frame on text. Chapter 19 swaps the representation for embeddings. Chapters 20–22 extend it across documents, images, multimodal inputs, and language model–driven workflows.
