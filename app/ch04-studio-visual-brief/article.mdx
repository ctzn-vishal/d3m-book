## The executive question: *why is growth uneven across stores?*

The chain is growing slightly. Inside that average, two of the three stores are doing something very different from the third. The capstone exercise is to find which is which, attach a *why* to each, and write up the recommendation in language a regional manager can act on. The eight tasks below trace the path from raw tables to a defensible Monday-morning memo. Each task corresponds to a chapter; each step builds on the one before.

The data is in Figure 1. Five tables, each at a specific grain. The exercise begins not with analysis but with reading the table list and writing down what one row means in each — exactly the practice from Chapter 1.

## The eight tasks

<Step number={1} title="Identify the grain of every table (Chapter 1)">

Before opening anything else, write a one-paragraph note: what does one row mean in `transactions`, in `customers`, in `products`, in `stores`, in `campaigns`? Figure 1 gives the answers, but a manager who has not internalized them will, by task 4, attempt a join at the wrong grain. Note especially that `campaigns` has multiple rows per customer — the duplicate-explosion hazard from Chapter 3 lives here.

</Step>

<Step number={2} title="Classify every variable (Chapter 2)">

For each column across the five tables, name the type: numeric, categorical, ordered categorical, date/time, geographic, boolean, or text. Pay particular attention to the looks-like-a-number-but-isn't traps: `customer_id`, `product_id`, `store_id`, and any ZIP-like field are categorical despite their numeric storage. Write the variable dictionary down once and reuse it across every task that follows.

</Step>

<Step number={3} title="Join the tables (Chapter 3)">

The business question is at the transaction grain — every action ultimately ties back to a purchase. Build the analysis table by left-joining `transactions` to `customers` (to pick up loyalty tier and city), to `products` (to pick up category and unit cost), and to `stores` (to pick up region and square footage). Do **not** join `campaigns` directly without aggregating first; the multiple rows per customer will explode the transaction count. Aggregate `campaigns` to one row per customer (e.g., `was_exposed_any` and `n_campaigns`), then join. Run the anti-join afterwards to surface any transactions whose customer or product is missing from the master files.

</Step>

<Step number={4} title="Create business metrics (Chapter 4)">

From the joined table, derive the metrics that will carry the memo. At minimum: revenue, AOV, repeat-purchase rate, gross margin (using `unit_cost` from `products`), revenue per active customer, and a 4-week rolling chain-wide revenue. Write a one-paragraph definition for each — what window it covers, what counts as "active," what counts as "repeat" — and keep that paragraph next to the chart that will display the number.

</Step>

<Step number={5} title="Reshape monthly sales (Chapter 4)">

Pivot the daily transaction-level data to a **long-format store-month panel**: one row per store per month, with revenue, transactions, and AOV per row. Six months × three stores = eighteen rows. Resist the urge to keep the data in a wide store-by-month spreadsheet for "ease of reading" — every chart you build downstream wants long format, and the wide form locks you out of plotting and modeling.

</Step>

<Step number={6} title="Build the dashboard storyboard (Chapter 5)">

Lay out the page in six panels: executive question at the top, KPI tile for chain-wide revenue with YoY change, line chart of the 6-month chain trend, sorted bar chart of revenue by store, drilldown panel for whichever store the bar chart surfaces as the breakout (or the laggard), recommended action at the bottom. Resist adding panels for completeness; resist showing every metric just because you computed it.

</Step>

<Step number={7} title="Write three descriptive insights">

Three sentences, each backed by a chart from the dashboard. Each one names *which store, which time period, which metric, which comparison*. Example shape: *"Store C (Suburban) grew transactions 47% over the last six months while AOV stayed flat at $4.85; the growth is from new customers, not larger baskets."* Avoid vanity claims (*"the chain is doing well"*); claim specifics that an executive could disprove with a different chart.

</Step>

<Step number={8} title="State what cannot be concluded causally">

This is the most important task and the one most often skipped. Everything you have done in steps 1–7 is **descriptive**. You can describe what is happening; you cannot, with what is in Figure 1, say *why* it is happening or what *would* happen if the regional manager changed something. Write one paragraph naming the limits. *"We do not know whether Suburban's growth is caused by the new university campus three miles away, by the Spring Latte campaign that was disproportionately targeted there, or by a competitor closing on the same block. Distinguishing these explanations requires either an experiment (Chapter 8) or a credible identification strategy (Chapter 11) — neither of which the current case pack supports."*

</Step>

## What good looks like

The deliverable is a one-page memo plus the dashboard. The memo names the question, summarizes the three descriptive insights from task 7, and closes with task 8's limits paragraph. The dashboard is the evidence the memo points to. Both should be readable in under three minutes by someone who has not seen the data. The temptation in a capstone is to demonstrate everything you have learned; the discipline is to use only what the question requires.

<Pitfall title="Skipping task 1, the grain">
The grain is the most boring and the most consequential part. **A student who skips writing it down spends task 3 fighting joins and task 5 fighting reshapes** that should have been a single keystroke each.
</Pitfall>

<Pitfall title="Treating tasks 4–5 as engineering">
The metric definitions in task 4 and the panel reshape in task 5 are *managerial* decisions, not engineering ones: the window for "active customer" is a choice, and so is the split between growth in new customers and growth in baskets. **Document each choice in the memo, in plain English, where the decision was made.**
</Pitfall>

<Pitfall title="Conflating descriptive with causal">
"Store C is growing because of the Spring Latte campaign" is a causal claim that nothing in Figure 1 supports; "Store C is growing, and the customers driving that growth disproportionately received the Spring Latte campaign" is descriptive and is supported. The two sentences sound alike and are not. **Reserve causal language for task 8, where the limits are stated explicitly.**
</Pitfall>

<Takeaway label="The bottom line">

Part I has taught a way of reading business data: grain first, structure second, types third, then operations, then quality, then visualization. The capstone is the moment where the sequence stops being theory and becomes a memo. Every chapter you have read shows up here as a task, and every task corresponds to a real decision the regional manager will defend on Monday morning. The chain is growing 2%. *Why* the chain is growing 2% is a question Part I lets you answer carefully, descriptively, with limits — and *which lever to pull next* is a question Part II is built to answer, by adding to the toolkit the one thing Part I deliberately withholds: a credible causal argument.

</Takeaway>
