<DropCap>

Historical data is the most attractive raw material in business analytics. It is already collected, virtually free, and often runs into millions of rows. It is also notoriously hard to interpret causally, for one fundamental reason: in historical data, the actions — prices, ads, promotions, layouts, offers — were not assigned randomly. They were chosen by managers responding to the very conditions we are trying to study.

</DropCap>

That purposeful decision-making makes the treatment variable **endogenous**: it carries information about the demand environment in addition to its own causal effect. This article works through the four standard sources of endogeneity, derives the omitted-variable-bias formula that ties them together, and shows the visible signature of confounding in a real scanner dataset.

---

## The Executive Question: What Did the Decision-Maker Know?

A regional manager observes that store managers cut pastry prices by half whenever afternoon traffic looks weak. A junior analyst regresses pastry sales on the discount flag and finds a *negative* correlation. The presented conclusion: "Discounts hurt sales."

The conclusion is wrong, and the reason is structural. The discount was triggered *because* expected sales were low. The historical data has an unobserved confounder built into how the action was chosen. Before treating a historical regression coefficient as a lever, every manager should ask:

> *What did the decision-maker know when they took this action, and how did that knowledge affect both the action and the outcome?*

That question, applied seriously, surfaces four recurring patterns.

## The Mathematics of Omitted Variable Bias

The four sources above all collapse, mathematically, into the same form. Suppose the true outcome model is

<Equation
  label="True model"
  latex={String.raw`Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i} + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i \mid X_{1i}, X_{2i}] = 0`}
/>

where <M>{String.raw`X_1`}</M> is the treatment of interest (price), <M>{String.raw`X_2`}</M> is some other driver of the outcome (seasonal demand), and <M>{String.raw`\beta_1`}</M> is the causal effect we want.

An analyst who does not observe (or forgets to control for) <M>{String.raw`X_2`}</M> runs the short regression

<Equation
  label="Short regression"
  latex={String.raw`Y_i = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 X_{1i} + u_i`}
/>

Standard OLS algebra gives a clean expression for what the short-regression coefficient is really estimating:

<Equation
  label="Omitted variable bias"
  latex={String.raw`\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widehat{\alpha}_1\right] \;=\; \underbrace{\beta_1}_{\text{true effect}} \;+\; \underbrace{\beta_2 \cdot \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_{1}, X_{2})}{\operatorname{Var}(X_{1})}}_{\text{omitted-variable bias}}`}
/>

The bias term is the product of two pieces, and both signs must be right to predict the direction of the bias:

1.  How the omitted variable moves the outcome — the sign of <M>{String.raw`\beta_2`}</M>.
2.  How the omitted variable moves with the treatment — the sign of <M>{String.raw`\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2)`}</M>, which is exactly the slope of a regression of <M>{String.raw`X_2`}</M> on <M>{String.raw`X_1`}</M>.

Memorize the 2×2:

  caption="Table 2. The sign of omitted-variable bias depends on the sign of two simpler relationships. Get them both right and you can predict whether your naive coefficient overstates or understates the truth."

    { key: 'cov', label: 'Cov(X₁, X₂)', align: 'left' },
    { key: 'beta2pos', label: 'β₂ > 0 (omitted variable raises Y)', align: 'left' },
    { key: 'beta2neg', label: 'β₂ < 0 (omitted variable lowers Y)', align: 'left' },
  ]}

    { cov: '− (treatment moves against omitted variable)', beta2pos: 'Bias < 0 — short coefficient too low', beta2neg: 'Bias > 0 — short coefficient too high' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout variant="definition" title="Omitted variable bias">
  The bias an OLS coefficient picks up when a regressor that drives the outcome is also correlated with the included treatment. The included coefficient is forced to absorb both its own effect and a share of the omitted variable's effect.
</Callout>

### Walking through a pricing example

Why does omitting season distort soup elasticity?

- **True elasticity** <M>{String.raw`\beta_1`}</M> is negative — higher price means lower volume.
- **Seasonal demand** <M>{String.raw`\beta_2`}</M> is positive — in winter, demand is higher at any price.
- **Pricing behavior**: retailers raise prices in winter when demand is high, so <M>{String.raw`\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) > 0`}</M>.
- **Bias sign**: positive × positive ⇒ bias is positive ⇒ short-regression coefficient is pulled *less negative* than the truth.

A manager who reads the biased coefficient sees customers as relatively insensitive to price, raises prices further, and watches profit collapse when the genuinely negative elasticity finally bites. The bug is not in the math; it is in pretending the omitted variable does not exist.

## Reverse Causality and Simultaneity in One Picture

Reverse causality is omitted-variable bias' close cousin: in the short regression, the residual <M>{String.raw`u_i`}</M> contains the demand shock that *triggered* the action. Now <M>{String.raw`X_{1i}`}</M> and <M>{String.raw`u_i`}</M> are correlated *because the manager looked at the demand shock before choosing the action*. The OLS estimate inherits whatever pattern of action-given-demand the manager was using.

Simultaneity is the most general case: two variables move together because each one depends on the other in equilibrium. Competitor pricing is the canonical example — our price depends on theirs, theirs depends on ours, and a shared cost shock moves both. None of OLS, fixed effects, or naive controls can untangle this without an instrument or a design.

## Data Case: Season as a Confounder in Soup Pricing

A short-run picture of the confounding pattern in real scanner data: Progresso soup volume against price, faceted by season. If season were unrelated to either price or volume, the two panels would lie on top of each other. Figure 3 shows that they do not.

<Figure width="screen-inset" caption="Figure 3. Log price and log volume for Progresso soup, faceted by season. Winter sits systematically higher (more soup sold at every price level), and the within-season slope is meaningfully different from the pooled slope. Pooling the two seasons without a seasonal control is a textbook omitted-variable trap.">
  <div className="not-prose">
    <SoupSeasonScatter data={data.soup} groupBy="season" />
  </div>
</Figure>

Two features of Figure 3 are diagnostic. First, the winter cloud sits visibly above the non-winter cloud — soup demand is higher at any given price in winter. Second, the relationship between price and volume is *steeper* within season than the line that would be fit across the pooled data, because the pooled line gets dragged toward the high-price, high-volume corner where winter prices and winter demand coincide.

The naive pooled regression returns an elasticity near <M>{String.raw`-3.21`}</M>. The within-season slopes, and the store fixed-effects model we will build in Chapter 11, return something closer to <M>{String.raw`-2.23`}</M>. The mechanical reason is exactly the omitted-variable formula above: omitting season — with <M>{String.raw`\beta_{\text{season}} > 0`}</M> and <M>{String.raw`\operatorname{Cov}(\text{price}, \text{season}) > 0`}</M> — pulls the price coefficient toward zero.

We will return to this case across Chapter 11 (regression) and Chapter 13 (pricing). For now, the picture is the lesson: where the demand environment moves with the action, the naive coefficient is biased in a predictable direction. Predicting the direction is the first defense; designing around the confounder is the second.

<Pitfall title="Controlling for everything in sight">
Adding controls is not free: a control that is itself a *consequence* of the treatment (a post-treatment variable) closes off part of the causal path you are trying to measure. **Choose the control set on substantive grounds — only variables determined before the treatment — before you fit.**
</Pitfall>

<Pitfall title="Pretending a time control fixes simultaneity">
Time controls absorb shared shocks, but they do not undo the fact that our price and a competitor's price are jointly determined in equilibrium. **When two variables are simultaneously determined, reach for an instrument or a design that breaks the loop — no amount of seasonality dummies will.**
</Pitfall>

<Pitfall title="Pooling regions with different baselines">
Pooling stores across regions hides large unmodeled level differences and drags the slope toward the high-volume corner. **Add a regional (or store) fixed effect — usually a cheap and decisive fix, built explicitly in the next chapter.**
</Pitfall>

<Takeaway label="For the manager">

Historical correlations are not causal facts. Before reading a historical coefficient as a lever:

- Map the process that generated the treatment. Who decided, on what information?
- Name the unobserved demand shocks that plausibly drove the action.
- Use the omitted-variable formula to predict the *sign* of the bias before running the regression.
- Choose a design or a control set that breaks the dependence between the treatment and those unobservables.

The next chapter takes that program seriously: regression as a tool for *isolating* an effect once the identification story is named.

</Takeaway>
