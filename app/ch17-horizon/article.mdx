It is tempting to end a book like this with a confident prediction. The honest move is to end it with a contradiction. As of 2026, agent-operated analytics sits squarely between two true stories: a wave of credible forecasts about autonomy arriving fast, and a pile of sober evidence that most attempts to deploy it fail. A manager who believes only the first becomes a cautionary statistic; one who believes only the second gets left behind. The skill is holding both — and the whole of this book has been training for exactly that kind of judgment.

## Two true stories at once

The bull case is real and well-sourced. Gartner projects that a third of enterprise software will embed agentic AI by 2028, up from under 1% in 2024, and that 15% of day-to-day work decisions will be made autonomously by then.<Cite id="gartner" sources={sources} /> The reality check is just as well-sourced. An MIT study found that about 95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots delivered no measurable profit impact;<Cite id="mit" sources={sources} /> S&P Global found the share of firms abandoning most of their AI initiatives jumped from 17% to 42% in a single year;<Cite id="sp" sources={sources} /> and RAND put the AI-project failure rate above 80%, roughly twice that of ordinary IT projects.<Cite id="rand" sources={sources} />

## Why most fail — and who doesn't

The failures are not mysterious. The MIT work diagnosed a &ldquo;learning gap&rdquo;: systems that never retain feedback or improve. It also found a sharp split in *how* organizations succeed — buying from specialized vendors worked about 67% of the time, while internal builds succeeded roughly half as often.<Cite id="mit" sources={sources} /> McKinsey's numbers point the same way: adoption is wide but shallow, with only 23% of organizations scaling even one agentic system and rarely beyond a function or two.<Cite id="mckinsey" sources={sources} /> The winners share a profile — narrow, workflow-embedded deployments with a clear owner and a tight feedback loop. The losers share one too: broad, unscoped &ldquo;let the AI figure it out&rdquo; ambitions.

## The durable lesson: own the contract

If one architectural idea survives the hype cycle, it is the one from [§17.2](/ch17-text-to-sql): the **semantic layer is the contract** that lets every agent in a chain resolve business logic identically. A governed metric definition — what &ldquo;active customer&rdquo; means, how &ldquo;margin&rdquo; is computed — is what keeps a fleet of agents consistent, auditable, and correct.<Cite id="cortex-docs" sources={sources} /> Models will keep changing; the contract is the asset that compounds. The firms that win the agentic transition will be the ones that treated their definitions as infrastructure long before the agents arrived.

## The reliability ceiling

It also pays to be precise about what agents still cannot do. Even text-to-SQL, the most mature data-agent capability, trails human experts: on the BIRD benchmark the best systems reach about 82% execution accuracy against a 93% human baseline — an 11-point gap that, on a metric that drives decisions, is enormous.<Cite id="bird" sources={sources} /> That ceiling is why autonomy is being deployed cautiously and *measured*. Anthropic's analysis of real agent usage found that 80% of agent tool calls came from agents with at least one safeguard in place and 73% appeared to keep a human in the loop, while only about 0.8% of actions looked irreversible.<Cite id="anthropic-autonomy" sources={sources} /> Autonomy is expanding — but unevenly, and with humans deliberately kept close to anything that cannot be undone.

## The analyst's job moves up a level

For the reader of this book, the most consequential shift is to the work itself. As agents absorb the mechanical tasks — writing the query, building the dashboard, running the model — the human moves &ldquo;above the loop.&rdquo; The scarce, durable skills become framing the question, owning the semantic layer the agents depend on, and judging whether an answer can be trusted.

## What it all converges on

Standardization is quietly doing the unglamorous work that makes the rest possible: MCP for agent-to-data access and A2A for agent-to-agent collaboration, both now under neutral governance, are turning a fragmented field into an interoperable stack.<Cite id="mcp-intro" sources={sources} /> And the products keep getting more autonomous — Databricks' Genie and Snowflake's Intelligence for governed analytics, OpenAI's ChatGPT agent operating its own computer and asking permission before consequential actions.<Cite id="genie" sources={sources} /><Cite id="chatgpt-agent" sources={sources} /> Put it together and the picture is not a robot replacing the analyst. It is the data-to-decision loop this book opened with — now with an agent able to turn each crank, and a human above it who still owns the question, the definitions, and the call.

<Callout variant="finding" title="The bet this book makes">
Agents will automate the production of analysis. They will not automate the *responsibility* for it. Someone still has to ask the right question, define the metrics honestly, and decide whether the evidence supports the action. That someone is the reader — and that judgment is what every chapter has been building.
</Callout>

<Pitfall title="Outsourcing the judgment along with the labor">
The seductive error of the agentic era is to let the system that produces the answer also be trusted to vet it. **An agent can write the query, run the model, and draft the memo — but the counterfactual, the threshold, and the decision to act remain a human's to own.** A firm that hands those over has not adopted AI; it has abdicated, and it will be among the 40% of agentic projects that get canceled when the bill and the mistakes arrive together.
</Pitfall>

<Takeaway label="The last word">

The agentic turn does not retire the discipline of data-driven decision-making — it raises the stakes on it. Agents make it trivial to produce a confident answer and no easier to produce a correct one, which means the skills this book has taught — reading a table honestly, demanding a counterfactual, naming what could be wrong, governing what ships — become *more* valuable, not less. The machines will operate the loop. Owning the question, the evidence, and the decision is the job that remains. That job is yours.

</Takeaway>
