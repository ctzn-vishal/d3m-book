<DropCap>

Estimating elasticity is not the goal — *setting a price* is. The bridge from a regression coefficient to a price recommendation is one of the most elegant results in microeconomics: the inverse-elasticity (or Lerner) rule, which says that the profit-maximizing markup over marginal cost is determined entirely by the absolute value of the own-price elasticity. The same rule also makes vivid the danger of acting on a confounded elasticity, because the markup formula amplifies bias in the elasticity estimate directly into the recommended price.

</DropCap>

This article derives the optimal markup rule, shows where the formula breaks down (and why that breakdown is informative), illustrates the markup curve graphically, and works through a data case that quantifies the cost of pricing on a naive elasticity estimate.

---

## The Executive Question: What Price, with What Guardrails?

A pricing analyst announces that the formula recommends cutting price from $3.50 to $2.00 because elasticity is <M>{String.raw`-2.00`}</M>. A senior manager should never accept this on its face. The right executive question is two questions at once:

> *What is the formal economic argument behind the optimal price, and what operational guardrails should bracket how we test it?*

The first half is the math we are about to derive. The second half is what separates a candidate test price from a national price change.

---

## Deriving the Optimal Markup Rule

Start with the standard profit function for a firm choosing a single price <M>{String.raw`P`}</M>, with constant marginal cost <M>{String.raw`MC`}</M> and demand <M>{String.raw`Q(P)`}</M>:

<Equation
  label="Profit"
  latex={String.raw`\Pi(P) \;=\; (P - MC) \cdot Q(P)`}
/>

Take the derivative with respect to price and set to zero (using the product rule):

<Equation
  latex={String.raw`\frac{d\Pi}{dP} \;=\; Q(P) + (P - MC) \cdot \frac{dQ}{dP} \;=\; 0`}
/>

Divide both sides by <M>{String.raw`Q(P)`}</M>:

<Equation
  latex={String.raw`1 + (P - MC) \cdot \frac{1}{Q} \cdot \frac{dQ}{dP} \;=\; 0`}
/>

Multiply the right-hand fraction by <M>{String.raw`P/P`}</M> so the elasticity <M>{String.raw`\epsilon = (dQ/dP)(P/Q)`}</M> appears explicitly:

<Equation
  latex={String.raw`1 + \frac{P - MC}{P} \cdot \epsilon \;=\; 0`}
/>

Rearrange to obtain the **Lerner index**: the profit-maximizing markup as a fraction of price equals the negative inverse of the elasticity:

<Equation
  label="Lerner index"
  latex={String.raw`\frac{P^* - MC}{P^*} \;=\; -\frac{1}{\epsilon}`}
/>

Solving for the optimal price:

<Equation
  label="Constant-elasticity optimal pricing rule"
  latex={String.raw`P^* \;=\; \frac{MC}{1 + \dfrac{1}{\epsilon}}, \qquad \text{valid for } \epsilon < -1`}
/>

Two readings of this formula are immediately useful:

- **As elasticity grows in magnitude** (<M>{String.raw`\epsilon \to -\infty`}</M>), the term <M>{String.raw`1/\epsilon \to 0`}</M> and <M>{String.raw`P^* \to MC`}</M>. In a hyper-competitive market the firm has no pricing power and must price at cost.
- **When <M>{String.raw`-1 < \epsilon < 0`}</M>** (inelastic zone), the denominator becomes negative or zero and the formula collapses — mathematically recommending infinite price. The right interpretation is that a profit-maximizing firm should never be in the inelastic zone: raising price would simultaneously raise revenue and reduce volume-driven costs. The formula breaking is the formula telling you the regression must be wrong.

## The Markup Curve, Visually

The relationship between <M>{String.raw`|\epsilon|`}</M> and the optimal markup is non-linear and steep at low values.

Moderately elastic categories (|ε| ≈ 2) carry meaningful markups. As |ε| approaches 1, the markup explodes — which is also where the formula stops being trustworthy.">

</Figure>

The curve does two pedagogically useful things. First, it makes vivid how much a small change in elasticity matters for the recommended price. Moving from <M>{String.raw`|\epsilon| = 2.0`}</M> to <M>{String.raw`|\epsilon| = 2.5`}</M> shifts the optimal markup from 100% over MC down to roughly 67%. Second, it shows how dangerous the formula becomes near <M>{String.raw`|\epsilon| = 1`}</M>: the markup goes to infinity in a way that is mathematically defined but operationally meaningless.

## Guardrails: The Formula Is a Generator, Not a Command

The optimal-pricing formula has exactly three inputs: own-price elasticity, marginal cost, and the assumption that the world is static. Each of those deserves a check before the recommendation leaves the spreadsheet.

| Guardrail | What it protects against | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Causal identification of <M>{String.raw`\epsilon`}</M> | Acting on a confounded elasticity that is wrong by a factor of 1.5× | Was the regression run with appropriate fixed effects and controls? Climb the regression ladder. |
| Stable marginal cost | Optimizing yesterday's cost while procurement costs are rising | Refresh <M>{String.raw`MC`}</M> regularly; recompute the price when inputs move materially. |
| Competitor reaction | Assuming rivals will hold price after a major move | Cross-reference with cross-price matrix; expect retaliation when cross-elasticities are large. |
| Customer trust | Short-term profit at the cost of long-term LTV | Run a holdout cohort to measure churn at the new price, not just immediate volume. |
| Empirical validation | Treating a model number as a real-world certainty | Stagger the rollout regionally; require A/B evidence before national deployment. |

The formula gives you a *candidate* price to test, not a price to set globally. That is the single most important framing to carry out of this chapter.

<Callout variant="caveat" title="Garbage in, garbage out — amplified">
  The bias in the elasticity estimate is amplified into the recommended price by the formula. A confounded elasticity of <M>{String.raw`-3`}</M> instead of a true <M>{String.raw`-2`}</M> recommends a markup of 50% instead of 100% — a halving of the per-unit margin. Always identify the elasticity carefully <em>before</em> using it in the pricing rule.
</Callout>

## Data Case: The Cost of Acting on the Naive Estimate

To put a dollar figure on the cost of pricing on a confounded elasticity, compare what the formula recommends under the naive and the well-identified estimates from Chapter 13.1.

A standard Progresso can has <M>{String.raw`MC = \$1.00`}</M>. Two competing elasticity estimates:

- **Naive bivariate OLS:** <M>{String.raw`\widehat{\epsilon} = -3.21`}</M>.
- **Store fixed effects:** <M>{String.raw`\widehat{\epsilon} = -2.23`}</M>.

Plug each into the formula:

<Equation
  label="Optimal price under the naive estimate"
  latex={String.raw`P^*_{\text{naive}} \;=\; \frac{\$1.00}{1 + 1/(-3.21)} \;=\; \frac{\$1.00}{0.6885} \;\approx\; \$1.45`}
/>

<Equation
  label="Optimal price under the well-identified estimate"
  latex={String.raw`P^*_{\text{FE}} \;=\; \frac{\$1.00}{1 + 1/(-2.23)} \;=\; \frac{\$1.00}{0.5516} \;\approx\; \$1.82`}
/>

The naive recommendation lands exactly at the *current* price — "we are already optimized, do nothing." The well-identified recommendation says current pricing is roughly 25% below optimum. The mechanical source of the bias is the omitted seasonality: retailers cut prices in low-demand months, so the naive regression sees discounts paired with the noise of low-demand weeks and overstates how much volume the discount actually produces.

The financial consequences are direct. At a marginal cost of $1.00:

- The naive model's "optimal" $1.45 gives a per-can margin of $0.45.
- The well-identified model's optimal $1.82 gives a per-can margin of $0.82 — an **82% increase in unit margin**.

Even after accounting for the volume loss implied by elasticity <M>{String.raw`-2.23`}</M>, the net change in total profit is strongly positive. The data case is the clearest possible illustration of why identification is the gating step in any pricing analysis: the formula is the same; the answer it produces depends entirely on the elasticity you feed it.

<Callout variant="finding" title="The identification step is the most valuable step">
  The same pricing formula returns wildly different recommended prices depending on whether the input elasticity was identified well or poorly. The technique you spent the most time learning — getting the elasticity right — is the technique that pays off largest in the pricing decision.
</Callout>

## What Can Go Wrong

Three traps recur in production pricing optimization work:

1.  **The singularity trap.** As <M>{String.raw`|\epsilon|`}</M> approaches 1 from above, the formula's denominator collapses and the recommended price explodes. Operationally, this signals either the regression is wrong or the firm needs to test small price increases cautiously — not implement the literal formula recommendation.
2.  **Stale marginal cost.** The formula assumes <M>{String.raw`MC`}</M> is known and current. In inflationary periods or supply disruptions, costs move week-to-week. Re-pricing every quarter on a six-month-old cost number leaves margin on the table.
3.  **The short-run / long-run gap.** Elasticity estimated from short-run price variation reflects the immediate substitution response. Long-run churn (customers gradually finding alternatives, brand erosion at higher prices) is not in the historical regression and not in the formula. Always pair the pricing recommendation with a customer-retention monitoring plan.

---

## Managerial Takeaway

The Lerner rule is short and decisive — and exactly that brevity makes its inputs important:

- Never plug a naive elasticity into the formula. The amplification of bias from elasticity to recommended price is direct and large.
- Treat the formula's output as a candidate test price, not a price to deploy nationally. Stage a regional pilot before rollout.
- Update marginal cost regularly. The recommended price scales linearly with <M>{String.raw`MC`}</M>; stale cost equals stale price.
