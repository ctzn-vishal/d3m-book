The chapter has three short ideas. Semantic search vs. keyword search. Vector databases as the infrastructure underneath. Embedding-based brand positioning as the bridge from structured surveys to unstructured text.

---

## The Executive Question

> Can we find documents by meaning instead of vocabulary, and can we read the resulting structure as competitive positioning?

The answer is yes for both, and the same primitive — nearest neighbour in embedding space — does the work in both cases.

---

## Semantic Search: Keywords vs. Meaning

A manager wants to find "customers angry about delivery delays." A keyword search returns only documents that contain "angry" or "delivery." A semantic search returns documents that share the *meaning*, regardless of vocabulary.

The mechanism is the §19.1 primitive applied in two steps:

1.  **At index time.** Embed every document in the corpus. Store the vectors in a database that supports nearest-neighbour queries.
2.  **At query time.** Embed the query with the *same model*. Retrieve the top-k documents whose vectors are nearest.

That's the entire architecture. The complexity is in scaling the nearest-neighbour search (approximate methods become essential beyond a few million documents) and in the prompt or interface that uses the retrieved documents.

---

## Vector Databases, Plainly

A vector database is what stores embeddings and answers "give me the k nearest neighbours of this vector, fast." The standard names — Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Milvus, Postgres+pgvector — implement variations of the same idea: index the vectors with an approximate-nearest-neighbour structure (HNSW, IVF, ScaNN), trade exactness for speed at scale, expose a simple query API.

Three managerial decisions when picking a vector database:

- **Approximate or exact?** Approximate is essential beyond a few million vectors; the precision loss is usually small and tunable.
- **Hybrid search?** Combining vector similarity with classical text filters (date, region, language) is often necessary. Most databases support this; pure-vector ones lose value quickly in production.
- **Where it lives.** Hosted service vs. self-hosted vs. embedded-in-existing-DB. The right choice depends on data sensitivity, scale, and team capacity.

The same caveat from §19.1 applies: the embeddings stored in the database are tied to a specific embedding model. Upgrading the model requires re-embedding the corpus.

## Embedding-Based Clustering

The same vectors that power search also power clustering. Once every document is a vector, K-means, hierarchical clustering, or DBSCAN — all from §16.1 — apply. The output is a segmentation of the corpus by meaning, not by vocabulary.

A few common business applications:

- **Complaint segmentation.** Cluster customer support tickets into themes; compare to the topic-model results from §18.5. The clusters are usually more stable across refits.
- **Lead segmentation from sales notes.** Cluster sales-call notes; identify customer personas the structured CRM fields don't capture.
- **Story discovery in social posts.** Cluster posts mentioning the brand; surface narratives the team didn't know existed.

The interpretive workflow is the same as §18.5: the algorithm produces clusters, the analyst names them, the manager decides which deserve action.

## Brand Positioning From Text

In §16.2, a perceptual map placed brands in a low-dimensional space using *survey ratings* (premium, affordable, cozy, convenient). The same kind of map can be built from *review text* using embeddings.

<Figure width="page-outset" caption="Figure 2. Two brand maps of the same six brands. Left: PCA of survey attribute ratings. Right: UMAP of review embeddings. The same competitive structure surfaces from two completely different evidence languages.">
  <div className="not-prose">
    <SurveyVsTextMap />
  </div>
</Figure>

The procedure for the text map:

1.  Collect reviews for each brand.
2.  Embed each review with a sentence/document embedding model.
3.  Average the embeddings within each brand to get a single brand vector.
4.  Reduce to 2D with UMAP or PCA for visualization.
5.  Read the resulting positions.

The reading habits are different across the two maps:

- **The survey map has named axes.** PC1 might be "value → premium." Useful for explaining the position.
- **The text map has neighbourhoods.** Brands close together are perceived similarly; the axes have no business meaning.

The strongest use is **triangulation**. If the survey map and the text map agree, the positioning is robust. If they disagree, that disagreement is informative — perhaps customers say one thing on surveys and another in reviews, or the survey questions don't capture the dimensions reviews actually emphasize.

<Callout variant="finding" title="The triangulation rule">
  A brand position you can see in both survey ratings <em>and</em> review embeddings is a position you can trust. Disagreement between the two is a research question, not a methodological failure.
</Callout>

## Two Useful Patterns Beyond Search

**Anomaly detection.** Documents whose embeddings are far from every cluster are candidates for human review. A support ticket that doesn't look like any previous ticket is exactly what an escalation queue should surface.

**Drift monitoring.** The centroid of "incoming reviews this week" can drift away from "incoming reviews last quarter" in embedding space. The drift is a richer signal than a single sentiment number — it captures what kind of language has changed, not just whether the overall tone shifted.

Both are restatements of the same primitive: distance in embedding space carries managerial information when interpreted carefully.

---

## What Can Go Wrong

- **Similarity ≠ relevance.** Two documents can be similar in language and irrelevant to the user's actual question. Production search systems pair semantic search with a relevance re-ranker — sometimes a smaller classifier, sometimes an LLM — that re-orders the top-k.
- **Embeddings inherit bias.** Brand positions and segment definitions inherit whatever was in the training corpus of the embedding model. Audit before high-stakes use.
- **Stale indices.** Re-embed periodically, especially when the embedding model changes or when product/category language shifts substantially.
- **UMAP misread as geometry.** The bridge in Figure 2 is convincing because the positions agree. Don't read individual distances on a UMAP plot as quantitative.
- **Mixing embeddings across models.** Restated from §19.1 because it keeps happening in practice: vectors from different embedding models do not live in the same space.

---

## Managerial Takeaway

Semantic search is the simplest, most operationally useful application of embeddings. Embedding-based clustering and brand positioning extend the same primitive to discovery and competitive analysis.

> *Search by meaning. Cluster by meaning. Position brands by meaning. Triangulate with the structured evidence you already have.*
